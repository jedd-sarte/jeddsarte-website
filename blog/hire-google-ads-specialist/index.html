Google Ads Hiring Guide

How to hire a Google Ads specialist without buying a black box.

The right person should do more than change bids and send reports. This guide shows what to look for, what to ask, and how to tell whether a specialist actually understands acquisition economics, conversion quality, and the account behind the dashboard.

Andreus Jedd Sarte · September 1, 2026 · Commercial Google Ads guide

If you are searching for a Google Ads specialist, you are probably not looking for someone who simply knows where the campaign settings live. You are looking for someone you can trust with budget, measurement, and a channel that can become expensive very quickly when the structure is wrong.

The strongest hiring decision starts by separating platform familiarity from actual account judgment. Google Ads is increasingly automated, but automation does not decide what the business should optimize for, which searches deserve budget, whether the conversion signal is trustworthy, or whether the landing page is turning expensive clicks into qualified opportunities.

Short version: hire the person who can explain the full path from search intent to conversion quality to business outcome. Do not hire based only on screenshots, CTR, certifications, or promises of more traffic.

What does a Google Ads specialist actually do?

A strong Google Ads specialist is responsible for the decisions that connect paid search to a business result. That usually includes campaign architecture, keyword and query strategy, ad messaging, budget allocation, bidding, conversion tracking, landing-page alignment, and ongoing analysis.

The exact scope changes by business model. A law firm may need to distinguish low-value inquiries from signed cases. A contractor may care about booked jobs by service area. An e-commerce business may optimize around purchase value, margin, product feed quality, and new-customer acquisition.

That is why the first question should not be, "Which bid strategy do you use?" It should be, "How would you decide what this account should optimize for?"

Seven things to evaluate before hiring

1. Can they define the real conversion?

Clicks are not the goal. Even form fills can be a weak goal if half the leads are unqualified. Ask what they would use as the primary conversion and what would remain secondary. A good answer should depend on your sales process, not on a generic template.

If tracking is unclear, the specialist should be comfortable auditing Google Ads conversion actions, GA4, Google Tag Manager, enhanced conversions, cross-domain journeys, or offline outcomes where relevant. See my conversion tracking service for the measurement layer I review alongside paid media.

2. Do they talk about search terms, not just keywords?

Your keyword list is what you ask Google to target. The Search Terms report shows what your budget actually bought. Someone managing Search campaigns should have a clear process for reviewing queries, adding negatives, finding new high-intent terms, and controlling match-type expansion.

3. Can they explain account structure in business terms?

Good structure creates control. Separate campaigns may be justified when service lines, geography, economics, budgets, or conversion behavior are meaningfully different. Over-segmentation can also be harmful if it starves campaigns of useful data.

The answer should not be "one campaign per service" by default. The specialist should explain what needs independent budget or bidding control and why.

4. Do they understand when automation should and should not be trusted?

Smart Bidding can be powerful when the conversion signal is reliable and the account has enough meaningful data. It can also optimize aggressively toward the wrong action when tracking is weak. Ask how they decide when to use Maximize Conversions, tCPA, Maximize Conversion Value, or tROAS, and what needs to be true before making that change.

5. Can they connect ads to landing pages?

A Google Ads specialist should not treat the website as someone else's problem. If the search term, ad, offer, and landing page are misaligned, account optimization alone may not fix performance.

Ask what they would review on the page. Strong answers usually include message match, mobile friction, CTA clarity, form length, page speed, proof, and whether different intents are being sent to the same generic page.

6. Do reports answer business questions?

A useful report should tell you what changed, why it changed, whether lead or purchase quality improved, what is being tested next, and where budget should move. A dashboard full of impressions, clicks, CTR, and average CPC is not enough.

7. Can they show relevant work without inventing certainty?

Case studies are useful, but look for reasoning, not just dramatic numbers. Good case studies explain the problem, the account decisions, the tracking or funnel constraints, and what changed. You can review examples in my Google Ads and paid media case studies.

Questions to ask on the first call

  • What would you want to know before touching the account?
  • How do you evaluate whether our conversion tracking is good enough for Smart Bidding?
  • How often do you review search terms, negatives, budgets, and conversion quality?
  • What would make you recommend restructuring an existing account?
  • How do you decide whether a lead is good enough to train bidding toward?
  • What do you need from our sales team, CRM, or website?
  • What would your first 30 days look like if you inherited this account?

Red flags when hiring a Google Ads specialist

  • Guaranteed ROAS or lead volume before seeing the account. There are too many unknowns in auction pressure, landing pages, offer quality, tracking, and sales follow-up.
  • CTR is presented as the main proof of success. CTR can be diagnostic, but it is not the business outcome.
  • No questions about conversion tracking. Bidding and reporting are only as good as the data behind them.
  • Every account gets the same structure. Different businesses need different control.
  • They avoid showing search terms or explaining changes. You should understand what your budget is buying.
  • They want ownership of your ad account. Your business should retain control of the Google Ads account, billing, analytics, and core assets.

These labels overlap. A freelancer can operate like a senior consultant. An agency can provide highly specialized expertise. The important question is who is actually making account decisions and how much context that person has about your business.

What should the first 30 days look like?

  1. Business and conversion audit: clarify the actual acquisition goal, economics, lead stages, purchase flow, and what data is available.
  2. Account audit: review structure, queries, budgets, bids, ads, audiences, conversion actions, and landing pages.
  3. Prioritized fixes: separate critical tracking or waste issues from lower-impact improvements.
  4. Controlled implementation: avoid changing every variable at once unless the account is fundamentally broken.
  5. Measurement baseline: document what will be compared going forward and what counts as a meaningful improvement.

FAQ

Hiring a Google Ads specialist

What should I look for when hiring a Google Ads specialist?

Look for clear thinking around business goals, search intent, conversion quality, account structure, tracking, bidding, and reporting. The person should be able to connect platform decisions to revenue or qualified acquisition.

Should I hire a specialist or an agency?

A specialist can be a strong fit when you want direct senior involvement and a focused scope. An agency can make more sense when you need a wider team across media, creative, development, and account service.

Do Google Ads certifications matter?

They are useful evidence of platform knowledge, but they should not replace account judgment, business understanding, tracking competency, or relevant work.
