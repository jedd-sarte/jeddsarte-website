How I Use Ahrefs, MCP and AI to Find SEO Opportunities That Actually Matter

Real keyword movements from a commercial fitness retailer, and why I don't let traffic numbers make the business decisions for me.

By Andreus Jedd Sarte   ·   October 21, 2026   ·   7 min read

A website can rank first for a popular search and still miss a more valuable opportunity a few positions lower. That's one of the things I pay attention to when reviewing SEO performance.

With USA Fitness Equipment Depot, a Texas-based retailer of new, used and refurbished commercial gym equipment, I use Ahrefs connected through the Model Context Protocol (MCP) to investigate rankings and opportunities in an AI-assisted workflow. The connection helps me work through the data. It doesn't tell me which customers the business should prioritize.

The problem with optimizing for traffic alone

Consider two searches: “hack squat vs leg press” and “used commercial gym equipment for sale.” The first suggests someone comparing exercises or machines. The second more clearly signals interest in purchasing equipment. Both deserve useful content, but they play different roles in the buying journey.

Ahrefs estimates that the first query brings the site about 763 organic visits per month, while the second brings about 76. Those are estimates for individual search terms, not conversions. The larger number isn't automatically the more valuable opportunity.

How the MCP connection changes the analysis

When I investigate a site's SEO performance, I need more than a list of winners and losers. I want to understand which ranking changes deserve action and which pages are already doing their jobs.

Connecting Ahrefs to an AI-assisted workflow lets me work through questions in sequence: Which relevant keywords are close to page one? Which URLs rank for them? Is the search informational or commercial? Does the page match the likely intent? Are competitors gaining ground? What is the smallest sensible change we can test?

That last question matters. If a collection page already ranks first for “refurbished gym equipment,” rewriting it extensively because an automated analysis suggested an improvement could create unnecessary risk.

The principle I follow: AI can help me explore the evidence faster. The decision to change a page still needs a business reason.

What search data cannot tell me by itself

Search volume and rankings cannot fully explain why someone hesitates to buy refurbished equipment. A commercial buyer might care about machine condition, available inventory, delivery, warranty details, or whether the equipment suits their facility. Those concerns need to be addressed through product information, page content and the actual customer experience.

Likewise, ranking first for an informational search doesn't mean those visitors are ready to purchase. I'd want to examine engaged sessions, inquiries and transactions before calling one group of keywords more profitable than another.

What improved, and what remains unproven

Ahrefs shows stronger estimated organic visibility and several improved commercial rankings during the period reviewed. That's useful evidence of SEO progress. It is not proof that AI or MCP generated those gains.

The connected workflow helps me investigate and prioritize, but SEO performance also depends on existing content, technical work, competitors, demand and search-engine changes. To quantify the incremental value of the AI workflow, I would need a reliable baseline for analysis time or a controlled comparison. To quantify customer impact, I would need verified conversion and revenue data.

The takeaway

My goal isn't to produce the longest SEO report. It's to figure out what the data means for the people searching and for the business serving them.

AI and MCP are useful because they make it easier to ask follow-up questions of complex data. They don't replace the work of understanding a buyer, evaluating a recommendation and taking responsibility for the result.
